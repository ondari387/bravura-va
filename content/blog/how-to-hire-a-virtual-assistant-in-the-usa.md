If you run a business in the United States, there's a good chance you've thought about hiring a virtual assistant. Maybe your inbox is out of control. Maybe customer emails are piling up while you're on sales calls. Or maybe you've realized you're spending your evenings on work someone else could do just as well.

Hiring a virtual assistant (VA) can be one of the highest-leverage decisions a business owner makes, but only if it's done thoughtfully. This guide walks you through the whole process, from deciding what to delegate to managing your assistant day to day.

## What a virtual assistant actually does

A virtual assistant is a professional who supports your business remotely. Unlike an in-house employee, they work from their own location using cloud tools: email, shared drives, project boards, your CRM and so on.

Common VA tasks include:

- Inbox and calendar management
- Customer support by email, chat or phone
- Data entry and CRM updates
- Research and list building
- E-commerce admin, such as product listings and order enquiries
- Social media scheduling and basic design
- Bookkeeping administration, like receipt capture and invoicing
- Project coordination and meeting notes

The common thread: work that's important, recurring and can be explained clearly.

## Step 1: Decide what you want to delegate

Before you look at a single candidate, get specific about the work. The most common reason VA relationships fail isn't the assistant, it's a vague role.

Try this exercise. For one week, keep a simple list of every task you do that takes more than ten minutes. At the end of the week, mark each task:

- **Only I can do this** (strategy, key relationships, final decisions)
- **Someone else could do this with guidance**
- **This probably shouldn't be done at all**

The middle category is your starting job description. You'll often find it contains several hours of work per week, sometimes far more.

## Step 2: Choose your hiring model

US businesses generally choose between three approaches.

### Hiring a freelancer directly

You post a job on a marketplace, review profiles and hire an individual. This gives you a lot of choice and direct control. The trade-off is that you handle vetting, onboarding, quality control and what happens if the person becomes unavailable.

### Working with a managed VA agency

An agency like [Bravura VA](/about/) helps scope the role, matches you with a suitable assistant and stays involved in onboarding and quality. You typically have one service agreement and a point of contact if anything needs adjusting. This suits ongoing support where continuity matters.

### Hiring an employee

For core, full-time roles that need to be in your office or deeply embedded in your company, an in-house hire may be right. It also brings payroll, benefits and employment obligations.

## Step 3: Consider location and time zones

Virtual assistants can be based in the US or overseas. Both can work well; the right answer depends on your needs.

If you need someone available during all of your business hours for live phone coverage, a closer time zone or an arranged shift is important. If much of the work can be done asynchronously (inbox triage, research, reporting, data entry) a time difference can actually be an advantage, because work happens while you sleep.

For example, Nairobi, Kenya is seven to eight hours ahead of US Eastern time. A Nairobi afternoon-and-evening schedule overlaps with an East Coast morning, while longer tasks can be completed overnight. ## Step 4: Write a clear role description

Whichever model you choose, write down:

- The tasks you want handled and how often
- The tools the assistant will use
- Expected working hours and any required overlap with your day
- How you'll communicate (Slack, email, Teams, WhatsApp)
- What "good" looks like: response times, accuracy, tone

A clear description attracts the right people and gives you a benchmark for evaluating performance later.

## Step 5: Vet for the skills that matter

Résumés only tell part of the story. When evaluating a VA, focus on:

**Written communication.** Ask for a short written response to a realistic scenario, such as replying to an unhappy customer. You'll quickly see their tone, clarity and judgment.

**Attention to detail.** A small, paid test task, cleaning up a spreadsheet, drafting a calendar plan, reveals far more than an interview.

**Tool familiarity.** Ask about the specific tools you use. Familiarity is helpful, but a capable assistant can learn new tools quickly.

**Reliability.** Ask about their working setup, backup internet and how they handle unexpected outages.

**Judgment.** The best assistants know when to act and when to ask. Ask how they'd handle a situation that falls outside the instructions.

If you work with an agency, ask how they assess these qualities and whether you can meet the assistant before starting.

## Step 6: Set up access securely

Security is one of the most overlooked parts of hiring a VA. A few simple practices go a long way:

- Give your assistant their own user accounts rather than sharing your logins.
- Use a password manager with shared vaults for any credentials you must share.
- Grant the minimum access needed for each task.
- Keep a list of everything the assistant can access, and remove access promptly when it's no longer needed.
- Put confidentiality expectations in writing.

If your business handles regulated data, health information covered by HIPAA, for example, assess those requirements before sharing anything. The [US Department of Health and Human Services](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/index.html) publishes guidance for covered entities and business associates.

## Step 7: Onboard deliberately

The first two weeks set the tone for the whole relationship. A good onboarding plan includes:

1. **A kickoff call** to explain your business, customers, priorities and preferences.
2. **Process walkthroughs.** Record short screen videos (Loom works well) of you doing each task once. Your assistant can turn these into written checklists.
3. **A starter task list** with low-risk, high-value work.
4. **Daily check-ins at first,** moving to weekly once things are running smoothly.
5. **A shared place to track tasks,** such as Asana, Trello or a simple shared document.

Expect a learning curve. Feedback early and often is far more effective than silently fixing mistakes yourself.

## Step 8: Manage for outcomes, not hours

Once your assistant is up to speed, shift your focus from supervising tasks to reviewing outcomes. Are customer emails answered within the agreed time? Is the CRM up to date? Did you start Monday with a clean inbox and a clear calendar?

A short weekly review (what went well, what's next, what's blocked) keeps everyone aligned without micromanagement.

## Legal and administrative considerations

Hiring a VA isn't usually complicated, but a few points deserve attention:

- **Worker classification.** If you hire an individual directly, make sure you understand whether they're an independent contractor or an employee. The [IRS provides guidance](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/independent-contractor-self-employed-or-employee) on the distinction for US purposes. Working with an agency means you contract with a business rather than an individual.
- **Data protection.** If your assistant handles customer data, consider any state privacy laws that apply to your business and follow your own data policies.
- **Marketing rules.** If your assistant helps with email outreach, follow the [CAN-SPAM Act](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/can-spam-act-compliance-guide-business).

This isn't legal advice, so check anything specific with a qualified professional.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Delegating chaos.** If a process is broken, delegating it just moves the problem. Simplify first.
- **Hiring for price alone.** The cheapest option often costs more in rework and management time.
- **Not giving enough context.** Assistants can't read your mind. Explain the "why" behind tasks.
- **Waiting too long.** Many owners hire a VA only once they're burned out. Starting earlier, even part-time, is usually better.

## In summary

Hiring a virtual assistant in the USA is less about finding a perfect person and more about setting up a good working system: a clear role, the right hiring model, secure access, thoughtful onboarding and regular communication.

Get those right and you'll get more than a few hours back each week, you'll get the space to focus on the work only you can do.
